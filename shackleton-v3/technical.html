Technical Overview

The Rover

The rover will have four-wheel skid steering, an extendable vertical mast for illumination and communications, and an exploration payload. The chassis will have solar panels and contain a battery and phase-change pack sized to permit one-hour sorties to the shadowed regions followed by a ten-hour recovery window.

◻

Architecture

Shackleton Rover is a compact four-wheel rover platform capable of traversing uneven regolith, shallow crater terrain, and transition zones between illuminated and shadowed regions. This architecture builds upon the UK’s long heritage in planetary robotics and autonomous systems, including technologies developed for the Rosalind Franklin Mars rover programme.

⌁

Communications

Communications will be S-band to the lander via a directional patch antenna mounted on a deployable vertical mast with yaw control. The mast will provide illumination and down-looking imagery for path planning in difficult terrain and low-angle lighting conditions. This type of mast is made in the UK by companies such as Rolatube.

⬤

Subsurface Exploration

The rover carries a sonicated penetrator system intended to investigate volatile-bearing material beneath the lunar surface without conventional rotary drilling. Stepped heating will drive liberated volatiles towards an ion-trap mass spectrometer, allowing discrimination between adsorbed water, pore ice, ice-cement, and associated compounds within the regolith.

Our Exploration Tools

Drilling requires large forces and generates a lot of torque. This can be reacted by heavy structures on the Earth, but in low-gravity environments such as the Moon a surprising problem arises. Without significant weight, forcing a drill downwards may simply drive a rover upwards, and rotating that drill may cause the rover to spin on the surface instead.

This is a problem. Our target - the water ice - may be underground, so drilling is essential. This is why many of our competitors are either seeking it only on the surface, or proposing rovers ten times larger than Shackleton: even in low gravity, a large mass will eventually prevail.

However, large rovers will be more expensive. They can only be in one place, and they cannot be delivered by fast commercial services. They are not a suitable model for our ambitions.

Therefore, Shackleton will be different. Our target mass is just 20kg, but we will equip it with unique technologies that will allow it to access the subsurface using much smaller forces. We will use ultrasonic penetrators to fluidise the lunar soil, so that we can penetrate it more easily; and we will use non-rotating drills to eliminate the need to spin heavy drillstrings in space.

Sonicated tools

When penetrating granular materials, everyone instinctively understands that you need to wiggle a tool to make it slide in more easily. This is because small movements disrupt the load-bearing chains of particles that transmit force from the tool to the far-field.

Research in the UK has indicated that this movement can be applied at the micron scale using ultrasonic vibration, fluidising granular materials and reducing the force required to penetrate a solid-state device by up to an order of magnitude.

This effect has been observed in ambient and hypergravity (see figure), in reduced gravity, and in vacuum. It has the potential to make the subsurface available to rovers such as Shackleton, which are at least an order-of-magnitude smaller than other currently-concieved platforms such as VIPER and LUPEX/Chandrayaan-5.

Pulse Elevator

Should the risk of ice-cemented ground be too high for a fluidising penetrator to operate, there are alternatives. One concept is the pulse-elevator, which uses an oscillating Tesla-valve geometry to lift granular materials without rotation.

This device is oscillated over a distance of around 10mm, at approximately 20Hz. On the downstroke, the device moves faster than the particles can fall, so they rise inside the features, while on the upstroke they are locked in place.

The oscillation can also serve as hammer drill in harder materials, with the vibration load being smooothed by a mass-damper. This means that a hole can be bored and the debris removed without any gross rotation, which solves the problem of reacting torque to the ground. The only requirement is that the device be gently dithered to prevent the bottom from becoming imprinted into the terrain.

The pulse-elevator can also be formed into any combination of horizontal and vertical paths, meaning that it is more flexible than a rigid-rotating auger. The concept has been tested using regolith simulants and in vacuum, where it continues to work well.

The pulse-elevator is a priority technolgy which already sits on the UK space exploration technology roadmap.
