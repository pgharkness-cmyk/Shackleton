Mission

Our Expedition

The Shackleton Rover is a proposed expedition to the permanently shadowed regions near the lunar south pole. The mission goal is to establish the first ground truth for water ice on the Moon. We know it is present, but we do not know if it is a surface frost, surface ice, or buried deeper in the subsurface. This is vital information that we must know before we can extract and use the resource to create new propellants in space.

◐

Why Water Ice?

The propellants make up more than 90% of the weight of any rocket launch. Water ice on the Moon could be broken into hydrogen and oxygen, which are ideal propellants, and used to refuel rockets so that they could travel much further than would otherwise be the case.

✦

Why the South Pole?

Water ice is thought to exist at the lunar south pole. Ground truth is scientifically valuable and strategically important because, while the ice has been detected from orbit, we do not know its form. It could be glacial deposits, frosting, or mixed with soil particles.

⚐

Why Shackleton?

Sir Ernest Shackleton's famous Endurance expedition has inspired ambition, leadership, and moral optimism for more than a century. This mission carries that ethos into space: endurance, discovery, and responsible exploration at a new polar frontier.

Mission Objectives

The primary objective is to investigate volatile-bearing regolith within the lunar south polar region. The rover would sortie from a sunlit landing area on the lip of the crater to selected shadowed exploration sites, chosen according to distance, risk, and scientific value.

Designed to operate for around one hour in the dark, the rover would use headlights to find its target while maintaining comms with is lander via a directional antenna on a vertically-deployed mast. There, it would deploy a shallow penetrator to examine the near-surface regolith, before returning to the sunlight to recharge. After around ten hours of recharging, the rover would be able to sortie to the next site.

Exploration Philosophy

The Shackleton Rover concept is will showcase responsible exploration. The mission draws deliberate inspiration from the history of Antarctic exploration: scientific ambition combined with endurance, cooperation, and long-term stewardship.

During the twentieth century, Antarctica evolved from a theatre of territorial competition into a peaceful international commons devoted primarily to science. The results have been clear, with global issues such as ozone depletion and wildlife conservation being addressed.

We will seek to demonstrate that a similar internationalist approach can work on the moon. For example our driver teams, based in Canada and Australia as well as the UK, will maintain constant operations on the moon from multiple time zones on earth.

Physical Presence

The success of the Antarctic Treaty System is guaranteed by the physical presence, on the ground, of those consultative states with the capability to operate in Antarctica. The same will likely be true on the moon.

The good news is that the UK already has significant convening power on the world stage. Shackleton Rover will provide the physical statement of intent that will be required to turn that foundation into a leadership role for good governance in space.

To underline this, our mission proposes to carry a small fragment of the RRS Discovery to the Moon, linking the great age of polar exploration with the beginning of a new era beyond the Earth. Just as pieces of the Wright Flyer have travelled to Mars aboard robotic explorers, Shackleton Rover will carry our heritage into space.

Mission Concept
1

Land near the lunar south pole

Arrive by CLPS or a similar commercial delivery service, targeting access to sunlit terrain near scientifically valuable shadowed regions.

2

Traverse to shadowed sites

Use a small rover platform to reach promising exploration locations that static landers cannot easily access.

3

Probe the regolith

Deploy a sonicated penetrator to investigate volatile-bearing material beneath the lunar surface.

4

Return knowledge to Earth

Provide data directly relevant to science, resource utilisation, mission design, and responsible lunar development.
