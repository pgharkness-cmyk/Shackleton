Mission

Our Expedition

A proposed robotic expedition to the lunar south pole, focused on the exploration of permanently shadowed regions near Shackleton Crater. The mission seeks to establish ground truth for water ice on the Moon: its distribution, accessibility, physical form, and relevance to future in-situ resource utilisation.

◐

Why the Moon?

The Moon is becoming a new frontier for science, infrastructure, and international coordination. A British surface mission would help focus national capability around a clear exploration goal while ensuring meaningful participation in the emerging lunar economy.

✦

Why the South Pole?

Water ice is thought to exist at the lunar south pole. Ground truth is scientifically valuable and strategically important because ice can be electrolysed to create hydrogen and oxygen - valuable rocket propellants - right where they are needed for future exploration.

⚐

Why Shackleton?

Sir Ernest Shackleton's famous Endurance expedition has inspired ambition, leadership, and moral optimism for more than a century. This mission carries that echo into space: endurance, discovery, and responsible exploration at a new polar frontier beyond the Earth.

Mission Objectives

The primary objective is to investigate volatile-bearing regolith within the lunar south polar region. The rover would sortie from a sunlit landing area on the lip of the crater to selected shadowed exploration sites, chosen according to distance, risk, and scientific value.

Designed to operate for around one hour in the dark, the rover would use lighting to find its target while maintaining comms with is lander via a directional antenna on a vertically-deployed mast. There, it would deploy a shallow penetrator to examine the near-surface regolith, before returning to the sunlight to recharge. After around ten hours of recharging, the rover would be able to sortie to the next site. This would be achieved through a mixture of teleoperation and mild autonomy. The value of our time on the moon would be maximised by having driver teams in Australia and Canada, covering each others' unsociable hours throughout the life of the mission.

The mission would seek to determine whether water is present as adsorbed molecules, pore ice, ice-cement, or other volatile-bearing material. The resulting data would help future mission designers understand how accessible lunar water ice may be, and whether it can support future propellant production, habitation, and wider solar-system exploration.

Exploration Philosophy

The Shackleton Rover concept is concerned not only with where humanity explores, but how exploration is conducted. The mission draws deliberate inspiration from the history of Antarctic exploration: scientific ambition combined with endurance, cooperation, and long-term stewardship.

During the twentieth century, Antarctica evolved from a theatre of territorial competition into a peaceful international commons devoted primarily to science. The results have been clear, with global issues such as ozone depletion and wildlife conservation being addressed.

We will seek to demonstrate that a similar internationalist approach can work on the moon. Our rover will be planned to operate with three driver teams, based in Canada and Australia as well as the UK, in order to maintain operations on the moon across different time zones on earth.

Physical Presence

The success of the Antarctic Treaty System is built and guaranteed by the physical presence, on the ground, of those nations which believe in its value. The same will be true on the moon.

The UK has enomous convening power on the world stage. Shackleton Rover would demonstrate positive intent, capability, and commitment at a time when the rules and norms of lunar activity are still being formed.

The mission also proposes to carry a small fragment of the RRS Discovery to the Moon, linking the great age of polar exploration with the beginning of a new era beyond the Earth. Just as pieces of the Wright Flyer have travelled to Mars aboard robotic explorers, Shackleton Rover seeks to carry a physical echo of our exploration heritage to another world.

Mission Concept
1

Land near the lunar south pole

Arrive by CLPS or a similar commercial delivery service, targeting access to sunlit terrain near scientifically valuable shadowed regions.

2

Traverse to shadowed sites

Use a small rover platform to reach promising exploration locations that static landers cannot easily access.

3

Probe the regolith

Deploy a sonicated penetrator to investigate volatile-bearing material beneath the lunar surface.

4

Return knowledge to Earth

Provide data directly relevant to science, resource utilisation, mission design, and responsible lunar development.
