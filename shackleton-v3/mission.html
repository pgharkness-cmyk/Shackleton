Mission

Our Expedition

The Shackleton Rover is a proposed expedition to the permanently shadowed regions near the lunar south pole. The mission goal is to establish the first ground truth for water ice on the Moon. We know it is present, but we do not know if it is a surface frost, surface ice, or buried deeper in the subsurface. This is vital information that we need to design the machines that will extract and then use this resource in space.

◐

Why Water Ice?

The propellants, fuel and oxidiser, make up more than 90% of the weight of any rocket launch. Water ice on the Moon could be broken into hydrogen and oxygen, which are ideal propellants, so that they would not need to be launched from Earth. Just as the early polar explorers relied on forward-deployed supply caches along their route, the ability to refuel our rockets in space would be transformational for the exploration of the solar system.

✦

Why the South Pole?

The Moon has permanently shadowed regions near the poles, such as craters, where sunlight never reaches. These regions become very cold and preserve ice for long periods of time. However, the lips of the same crater can be exposed to permanent sunlight. We can use the sunlight to generate the electricity we need to explore the shadowed regions, and subsequently to electrolyse the ice into useful propellants.

⚐

Why Shackleton?

Sir Ernest Shackleton's Endurance expedition has inspired ambition, leadership, and moral optimism for more than a century. This mission carries that ethos into space. To underline this, our mission will carry a small fragment of the RRS Discovery to the Moon. Just as pieces of the Wright Flyer have travelled to Mars aboard robotic helicopters, Shackleton Rover will carry our heritage into a new polar region in space.

Economic Impact

Many nations are planning their first lunar enterprise, and many of them will need expertise in prospecting, refuelling, and autonomous operations. Shackleton Rover will develop the UK as a leader in all three. This will position us to take advantage of an emerging lunar economy that is estimated to be worth around $5bn, in Europe alone, in less than 10 years' time.

However, we need to move quickly. We have a short period of time during which an scientific and economic outcome of global significance can still be delivered by a small rover making use of our specialised exploration tools. We are also at the point where lunar regulation is starting to take shape, and we have an opportiunty to lead by example. This opportunity cluster will not arise again.

Exploration Philosophy

The Shackleton Rover concept is will showcase responsible exploration. The mission draws deliberate inspiration from the history of Antarctic exploration: scientific ambition combined with endurance, cooperation, and long-term stewardship.

We will seek to demonstrate that a similar internationalist approach can work on the moon. For example our driver teams, based in Canada and Australia as well as the UK, will maintain constant operations on the moon from multiple time zones on earth.

Physical Presence

In the view of the signatories of the Antarctic Treaty System, Antarctica is as an international commons devoted primarily to science. The UK is a founding member of this system, and its success has been clear: global issues such as ozone depletion and wildlife conservation have been successfully addressed.

The Antarctic Treaty System is itself guaranteed by the physical presence, on the ground, of those consultative states (such as the UK) which have a significant capability to operate independently in Antarctica.

We can achieve the same outcome on the Moon. The UK has the convening power, on the world stage, to make the case for a new global commons which does not prohibit the pursuit of progress. Shackleton Rover will provide the physical statement of intent that will be required to turn that ambition into reality.

Mission Concept
1

Land near the lunar south pole

Arrive by CLPS or a similar commercial delivery service, targeting access to sunlit terrain near scientifically valuable shadowed regions.

2

Traverse to shadowed sites

Use a small rover platform to reach promising exploration locations that static landers cannot easily access.

3

Probe the regolith

Deploy a sonicated penetrator to investigate volatile-bearing material beneath the lunar surface.

4

Repeat

Return to the sunlight to recharge batteries and then sortie to the next exploration site.
